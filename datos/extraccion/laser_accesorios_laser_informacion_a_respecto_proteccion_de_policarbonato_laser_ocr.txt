The subject of laser safety is concerned with the safe exploitation of the
tremendous benefits that the use of laser technology can provide- Laser safety
should be seen as enabling, not constraining, and some understanding of laser
safety is important for all who use or work with laser equipment. As lasers have
developed, so has our understanding of the hazards that they can present and of
the precautions that are necessary in order to ensure their safe use. Part One of
this book gives an overview of the main essentials of laser safety, and Will also
describe What a laser actually isy Part T wo discusses sorne Of the background
issues in rather more detail, whereas Part Three considers the safety Of Some
specific applications of laser technology.
The first laser was based on a valuable but othenwise not very useful material
ruby. To most people ruby is a gemstone, favoured because of its relative
rarity and intrinsic beauty. Seen in the cold light of science ruby is an impure
crystal formed from the mineral corundum (aluminium oxide) in which a few of
alumimum atoms — less than 1% — have been replaced by those of
chromium. It is this presence of chromium as an impurity, absorbing broad
bands of light in the violet, green and yellow regions of the spectrum but
transmitting red together with some blue, that gives ruby its characteristic Pink
colour. An understanding of why a ruby crystal has this reddish appearance tells
us quite a lot, in fact, about the characteristics of light and its interaction with
us quite a lot, in fact, about the characteristics of light and its interaction with
matter, something that is at the heart of the subject matter of this book It
explains how atomic and molecular processes govern the optical properties of
materials and are responsible for the production of light and for its ultimate
absorption. It is absorption ofsuffciently high levels of light in the tissues of the
body that creates the main hazard posed by lasers.

In many laser applications, total enclosure of the beam is not a realistic option,
as it is necessary for people to work with access to an open beam. Even so, when
long beam paths are necessary, as much of the beam path as possible should be
enclosed, and screens should be used as appropriate to limit the area over which
exposure to laser radiation is possible. Such laser work should be carried out
within a designated laser controlled area, often a separate room, which has
restricted access limited to those whose presence is necessary. Appropriate
warmng signs should be displayed and, where class 3B or class 4 laser
equipment is being used, doors giving access into the laser area should normally
be interlocked so that laser emssion is terminated when opened. ThiS prevents
the exposure ofunprotected persons who may enter the laser area inadvertently.

Absorbing filters: advantages
The incident laser radiation is absorbed, and so is not refiected to transfer the
hazard elsewhere,
The filter properties are distributed throughout the material and so surface
defects, such as scratches, do not compromise filter performance.
The protective properties of the filter are not dependent on the angle at which
the laser beam is incident
Absorbing filters: disadvantages
Filters using organic dyes can be subject to fading and the consequent loss of
their protective propertiesm
For protection against laser radiation in or near the vislFe band, it can be
dificult to obtain a Sharp enough cut-off to permit good overall visual
performance*

Dielectric coated filters: advantages
