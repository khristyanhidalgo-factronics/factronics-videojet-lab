Product Marking Techniques
Unlike an ink or pigment based coders that apply a new material onto a substrate, lasers mark on products by altering the sub -
strate itself.
Ablation
Ablation is the removal of a layer to reveal contrasting layer below. The topmost layer of film or paint on the substrate is vapor -
ized.

Thermal or Chemical Change
In a thermal/chemical change technique, light and heat from the beam interact with the substrate to alter it at the surface
without changing its texture or removing substrate. On metals this is occurs as a function of surface oxides forming on the sub -
strate surface at the point of beam contact. Similarly on plastics, the beam spurs molecules of the substrate to react and change
color. If the plastic does not react, dyes or additives may be added to the plastic medium before formation. The beam will react
with those additives during marking.

Physical Change - Surface Modification
Lasers may mark on substrates by burning away substrate to a target depth (in the tens of microns). A trench is left behind in the
shape of the marked characters or graphics. Substrates that are too thin for engraving (e.g. plastic bottles) may simply be mod -
ified at the surface causing the formation of bubbles that raise up rather than leave behind a trench. Glass does not burn and is
marked by crazing , or shattering, at the point of beam contact.
Marks in Glass Substrates Sharpen Over Time
Since marks on glass substrates rely on crazing and not vaporization, the removed substrate does not always
immediately fall away. This means that at first a mark on the glass substrate may appear faint but will
sharpen as the substrate jostles on a conveyor and the tiny shards fall away.
Engraving
When engraving, a superficial incision removes a few microns of material. This occurs via melting/vaporization (metal), partial
burning (thermoplastic), or crazing (glass).

Substrate Modification
When marking by substrate modification, the beam causes an additive effect on the substrate rather than a reductive one as
occurs in engraving. The surface is momentarily softened and then resolidifies. While softened, impurities such as oxides or
gasses are released that alter the color or texture of the substrate.

How Does a Laser Work?
Laser beams are the product of energy discharged from atoms as photons. This energy emits as electrons orbiting the nucleus of
the atoms decay to a lower energy level. The energy that formerly kept that electron moving at a higher orbit converts to a
photon.
Emissions may be spontaneous or stimulated . Spontaneous emissions occur naturally based on the interaction of excited atoms
in the electromagnetic field in their environment but are incoherent. Stimulated emissions occur when an incoming photon of a
specific frequency interacts with an excited atomic electron causing it to decay to a lower energy level. This interaction is called
absorption .
Ordinarily absorption produces no photons as the electrons simply raise to a higher energy level. Lasers work because the envir -
onment inside the beams source is such that instead of raising to a higher energy level, electrons instead decay and the dif -
ference in energy is released as a second photon of the same phase, frequency, polarization, and direction of travel.

Laser Wavelengths
Lasers beams, being light and therefore a form of electromagnetic radiation, can be produced at varying wavelengths on the
EM spectrum .
Wavelength is the distance between one peak or crest of a light wave and the next peak or crest. It is equal to
the speed of the wave divided by its frequency, and to the speed of a wave times its period.
Electromagnetic radiation has varying effects on people and things. The radio waves, light waves, and X Rays are all
EM radiation. Radio Waves are harmless to people, light is dangerous only at some exposures and wavelengths, and X Rays are
dangerous at more than minimal occasional exposure.
F IGURE 1- 11: Where laser radiation wavelengths fall on the electromagnetic spectrum.
The primary wavelengths of laser radiation for current commercial applications fall within the Ultraviolet (UV), visible, and
infrared ranges of the spectrum. UV laser radiation wavelengths range between 180 and 400 nm. Visible light wavelengths range
between 400 and 700 nm. The infrared range of the EM spectrum consists of radiation at wavelengths between 700 nm and 1
mm.

Effects of Laser Radiation on Biological Tissue
Laser radiation absorbed by the skin penetrates only a few layers. In the eye, visible and near infrared radiation passes through
the cornea and is focused on and absorbed by the retina. It is the wavelength of the light that determines the visible sensation of
color: violet at 400 nm, red at 700 nm, and the other colors of the visible spectrum in between. When radiation is absorbed by bio -
logical tissue the effect on that tissue is either photochemical , thermal , or mechanical .
l In the ultraviolet range the effect is primarily photochemical.
l In the infrared range the effect is primarily thermal.
l In the visible range both effects are present.
l When the intensity of the radiation is sufficiently high, the result will be damage to the absorbing tissue.

Radiation Exposure Hazard - Eye
F IGURE 1- 12: Illustrated visual impact of eye damage caused by laser radiation.
To understand the possible health effects, it is important to understand the functions of the major parts of the human eye.
The Human Eye
The cornea is the transparent layer of tissue covering the eye. Damage to the outer cornea may be uncomfortable (like a gritty
feeling) or painful, but will usually heal quickly. Damage to deeper layers of the cornea may cause permanent injury.
F IGURE 1- 13: Parts of the human eye.
The cornea is a clear, dome- shaped surface that covers the front of the eye. It is the first and most powerful lens in the eye's
optical system. To keep it transparent the cornea contains no blood vessels. Tears that flow over it and aqueous humor in the
chamber behind it keep it nourished. When you hear of eye transplants, it is the cornea that is being replaced. The cornea can be
damaged by accidents, infections, and genetic defects.
18
Rev AA

Safety & Laser Concepts
The pupil is the black circle in the center of the eye, and its primary function is to monitor the amount of light that comes into the
eye. When there is a lot of light, the pupil contracts to keep the light from overwhelming the eye. When there is very little light, the
pupil expands so it can soak up as much as possible.
The iris is the colored part of the eye. Although it might seem purely cosmetic, the iris actually functions to adjust the size of the
pupil. It has muscles that contract or expand depending on the amount of light the pupil needs to process images.
The lens exists behind the pupil and is responsible for allowing your eyes to focus on small details like words in a book. The lens is
in a constant state of adjustment as it becomes thinner or thicker to accommodate the detailed input it receives. With age, the
lens loses a lot of its elasticity which often results in cataracts and presbyopia, i.e. nearsightedness, because the lens cannot
adjust as well to its surroundings as it used to.
The vitreous humour is a gel- like substance that helps to keep the eyeball in its proper, circular shape. This is the area in the eye
where floaters develop as pieces of the vitreous humor clump together and cast shadows onto the retina. With age, the vitreous
humor begins to shrink and can cause problems like posterior retinal detachment or retinal tears.
The retina is the area at the back of the eye that receives the refined, visual message from the front of the eye, and it transmits
that visual message to the brain using electrical signals.
The sclera is the white part of the eye, and its main function is to provide strength, structure, and protection for the eye. The
sclera contains blood vessels that can tell an eye doctor a lot about the overall state of your health.

l Ultraviolet and low energy far- infrared radiation can injure the epithelial layer of the cornea; a condition that is painful and
visually handicapping. At lower powers, this injury is primarily due to a photochemical reaction. A latency period of hours
may exist between the time of exposure and the development of the corneal pathology. Minimal corneal lesions heal within
a few days, but meanwhile hinder vision.
l High energy far- infrared radiation is mainly absorbed by the cornea producing immediate burns at all corneal layers. An
infrared laser can produce a burn resulting in immediate visual incapacitation and may lead to permanent corneal scarring.
Very high energy radiation can perforate the cornea which may result in complete loss of the eye.
l Laser light in the visible to near infrared spectrum (i.e., 400 - 1400 nm) can cause damage to the retina resulting in scotoma
(blind spot in the fovea). This wave band is also know as the "retinal hazard region". Exposure to the Q- switched Nd:YAG
laser beam (1064 nm) is especially hazardous and may initially go undetected because the beam is invisible and the retina
lacks pain sensory nerves. Photoacoustic retinal damage may be associated with an audible "pop" at the time of exposure.
Visual disorientation due to retinal damage may not be apparent to the operator until considerable thermal damage has
occurred.

hands, and arms are the most likely skin surfaces to be exposed.

l Stratum Corneum (Dead Layer)
The stratum corneum is the outermost layer. It consists of flattened, dead epidermal cells that protect the living tissue from
water loss, injury from physical objects, and radiant energy. The thickness is 8- 20 μm except on the soles of the feet and the
palms of the hands where it is 500- 600 μm.
l Epidermis
The epidermis is the outermost layer of living tissue, where the tanning process takes place. It has a relatively uniform thick -
ness of about 50- 150 μm.
l Dermis Corium
The dermis corium is made up largely of connective tissue which gives the skin its elasticity and supportive strength.
Included in this layer are nerve cells, blood vessels, and lymphatic glands. The thickness of this layer varies over the body
from 1- 4 mm.
l Subcutaneous Tissue
The subcutaneous tissue is made up mostly of fatty tissue serving as insulation and as a shock absorption medium. The
thickness of this layer varies according to the area of the body as well as from person to person.
Dermal Damage by Radiation Varies by Wavelength
Laser effects on tissue depend on the power density of the incident beam, absorption of tissues at the incident wavelength, time
beam is held on tissue, and the effects of blood circulation and heat conduction in the affected area.

Immediate Effects of Exposure
The immediate effect of exposure to laser light above the biological damage threshold is normally tissue burns. Injury to the skin
can be caused by either temperature elevation in skin tissues or from a photochemical effect (e.g., "sunburn") from excessive
levels of actinic ultraviolet radiation.

Delayed Effects of Exposure
The possibility of adverse effects from repeated or chronic laser irradiation to the skin has been suggested, although it is normally
discounted. Only optical radiation in the ultraviolet region of the spectrum has been shown to cause long- term, delayed effects
such as accelerated skin aging and skin cancer. At present, laser safety standards for exposure of the skin attempt to take these
adverse effects into account.
Burns, skin cancer and acceleration of skin aging are possible from exposure to laser radiation. Chronic exposure can cause
increased pigmentation.

Classes of Fire
Fires are classified as one of four types.
Class A - Ordinary Combustibles
Most common fire. A burning house is a class A fire.
Class B - Flammable Liquids
Liquid combustibles such as paint strippers, solvents, fuels, oil, or grease.
Class C - Electrical Equipment
Additional danger from electrical shock. Power equipment OFF immediately.
Class D - Combustible Metals
Combustible metals such as magnesium, aluminum, and metallic sodium. Metals burn easier in powder or chip form.
DO NOT apply water to a Class D fire as this can result in an explosion.
Fire Prevention & Safety
l Always store flammable liquids in approved cabinets.
l Never operate the laser system on unattended material.
l Always keep the area around the machine clean and free of clutter, combustible materials, explosives, or volatile solvents

Most absorbing materials satisfy the Beer- Lambert Law.
An isolated atom scatters light because the electric field of the incident light wave forces the electrons in the atom to oscillate
back and forth about their equilibrium position. By the laws of electromagnetism, when a charge changes its velocity, it emits
radiation.

Light is emitted uniformly in all directions in the plane ∞ to oscillation, but decreases in amplitude as the viewing angle shifts
away from that plane.
l Reflection occurs when light is scattered in the opposite direction of incident light.
l Refraction is a phenomenon in which light is scattered in the forward direction and combines with the incident beam. The
physical effect of this combination is to make the transmitted light appear as though it has traveled more slowly through
the sample than through a vacuum.
l Incident ray in physics, a ray of light which hits a surface.

the power in the reflected ray, and any power absorbed at the surface.

In the case of free propagation of a laser beam, special attention should be placed as the vision of the radiation is directly reflec -

Reflection is the return of radiant energy (incident light) by a surface, with no change in wavelength.

Specular vs Diffuse Reflection
F IGURE 1- 21: The difference between light reflected on different surfaces.
Reflection off of smooth surfaces such as mirrors or a calm body of water leads to a type of reflection known as specular reflec -
tion . Reflection off of rough surfaces such as clothing, paper, and the asphalt roadway leads to a type of reflection known as dif -
fuse reflection . Whether the surface is microscopically rough or smooth has a tremendous impact upon the subsequent
reflection of a beam of light. The diagram below depicts two beams of light incident upon a rough and a smooth surface.
A light beam can be thought of as a bundle of individual light rays which are traveling parallel to each other. Each individual
light ray of the bundle follows the law of reflection. If the bundle of light rays is incident upon a smooth surface, then the light
rays reflect and remain concentrated in a bundle upon leaving the surface. On the other hand, if the surface is microscopically
rough, the light rays will reflect and diffuse in many different directions.
Specular reflections are mirror- like reflections and can reflect close to 100% of the incident light. Flat surfaces will not change a
fixed beam diameter only the direction. Convex surfaces will cause beam spreading, and concave surfaces will make the beam
converge.
Diffuse reflections result when surface irregularities scatter light in all directions. The specular nature of a surface is dependent
upon the wavelength of incident radiation. A specular surface is one that has a surface roughness less than the wavelength of
the incident light. A very rough surface is not specular to visible light but might be to IR radiation of 10.6 µm from a CO2 laser.
Why a rough Surface Diffuses a Light Beam
For each type of reflection, each individual ray follows the law of reflection. However, the roughness of the material means that
each individual ray meets a surface which has a different orientation. The normal line at the point of incidence is different for dif -
ferent rays. Subsequently, when the individual rays reflect off the rough surface according to the law of reflection, they scatter in
different directions. The result is that the rays of light are incident upon the surface in a concentrated bundle and are diffused
upon reflection.
Counter Measures
l Make sure that the working distance is set correctly before marking any reflecting material. Use a weakly reflecting material
for testing, e.g. laminated paper.
l When marking reflecting material try not to mark in the center of the marking field. Position the product so that the mark -
ing is in the edge of the marking field.
l When setting the laser power start with the maximum value and reduce the power until the marking result is satisfying. If
no marking is visible at maximum power the material cannot be marked using this laser.

Nominal Hazard Zone

itself. In other words, the NHZ for you will be derived at the end of your safety calculations, and thereafter will be most useful to
you for planning control measures in you laboratory.

The dual detect setup works by monitoring the breaking of each beam and the time between these occurrences. The first trigger
detects the object and starts counting. When the object reaches the second trigger, the counting stops and the calculation for the
speed of the object begins. From this count and the known distance between both triggers, the system determines the speed of
the object on the conveyor belt.
When to Use Dual Detect
The dual detector will not be appropriate for all applications. Consider the following requirements:
l Gap between products: Minimum 40 mm gap
l Constant belt speed: Changes in speed while the product being sensed will lead to inaccurate placement.
l Type of ambient light: Overhead florescent tube or LED office lighting is best. No direct, intense sunlight
or incandescent lighting should shine onto the front of the sensors.
l Product Shape: The line speed sensor works by detecting a difference between nothing being there (low
amounts of infrared light reflected back into the sensor) and something being there (large amounts of
infrared light reflected back). The sharpness of that transition is provided by the shape and material of the
item to be printed onto.

l Enter or Measure Fixed Speed
Not applicable when an encoder will be used. Enter a fixed line speed. Used when conveyor speed is constant and no
encoder is being used. This is only recommended if conveyor speed will be steady and there will be no ramp up or ramp
down in speed. It is recommend that you use an encoder on variable line speed applications.
l Measure Line Distance
For applications using an encoder. Follow the on screen instructions for making a mark on the conveyor and belt and
measuring how much the mark moves after at least one rotation of the encoder. The conveyor must be moving extremely
slowly during measurement and no alterations can be made after entering the distance traveled. This is not a preferred
method of line setup.
l Enter Product Size
For applications using an encoder and product detector. Follow the onscreen instructions to enter product size. When the
conveyor moves the product past the detector, the number of pulses received while traveling past the detector is used to
calibrate pulses per millimeter. The conveyor must be moving extremely slowly during measurement and no alterations
can be made after entering the distance traveled. This is not a preferred method of line setup.
l Measure Line Speed
The Measure Line Speed method requires use of a tachometer on the line while moving and that you then enter the meas -
ured speed into the field on screen. Do not vary the speed of the line during this setup. In this method, the reference speed
and number of pulses detected become a benchmark for calculating other speeds based on number of pulses detected in
a given time period.
l Configure Manually
For use with applications using an encoder. Enter the number of pulses per rotation (resolution of encoder) and if using a
friction wheel enter the circumference of the wheel. If not using a friction wheel then enter the distance the line travels
during one revolution of the encoder. Any variations from the actual distance of travel or circumference of the wheel will
affect spacing in print characters. This is the preferred setup for use in encoder applications because adjustments may be
made to the Distance per Rotation setting after initial entry.

Dual Detect Setup
Using the double light barrier it is possible to detect the product and the product’s speed without a rotary encoder. The sensor
automatically adjusts the line speed for each separate product. This allows for a variable speed belt and for users to move the
printer from line to line more easily.
Important considerations for using a Double Light Barrier are:
l Gap between products: Minimum 40 mm gap
l Placement Accuracy : Although the double light barrier can accommodate variable speeds, for the best results the
product must come past the laser marking head and sensor consistently. A constant belt speed is key to getting the best
possible marking result as changes in belt speed between the product crossing the double light barrier and then reaching
the marking head negatively impact mark placement and marking quality. The double light barrier should be placed as
close as possible to the marking field borders to use the latest, real speed information for the marking.
l Sensor Calibration : Each light sensor should be calibrated equally for necessary distance. That distance must not exceed
the depth of the conveyor. Through the turn potentiometer, it is possible to calibrate the product distance and sensitivity
for needed product surface.
l Type of ambient light: Overhead florescent tube or LED office lighting is best. No direct, intense sunlight or incandescent
lighting should shine onto the front of the sensors.
l Product Shape: The line speed sensor works by detecting a difference between nothing being there (low amounts of
infrared light reflected back into the sensor) and something being there (large amounts of infrared light reflected back).
The sharpness of that transition is provided by the shape and material of the item to be printed onto.
l Material Reflectivity : The speed sensor sensitivity must be set correctly. For more IR reflective products, a lower sens -
itivity is recommended. For less IR reflective products, a higher sensitivity is recommended to minimize the likelihood of
double triggers.

There is a round cap on the front housing of the laser. This is not a filter and the opening is blocked by foam. Although the piece

Filter Mat Identification
The filter mats for the side and the rear air filters are a different shape. The air filter for the rear has the corners notched out to fit
around the mounting screws for the fan.
Orientation of the Side Filter Mat
There is a preferred orientation for the side air filter mat in order to achieve longest time between clogging. The Air Inlet filter
(side of laser) needs to be installed with the fluffy side of the filter mat toward the outside and the stiffer side toward the inside.
This way, large particles are caught in the fluffy region while smaller particles are caught on the more densely packed side. If
mounted the other way around, the efficiency of the filter would still be the same, but it will clog faster.

Lens Cleaning
The laser scan lens is located at the marking head. It may become contaminated by dust or floating particles. Contamination of
the laser scan lens can lead to damage of the laser scan lens and will gradually reduce the quality of the marking. You should
therefore clean the lens regularly.
Generally just the outwardly facing side of the laser scan lens requires cleaning, but check both sides for contamination and
clean as necessary.
Personal Injury - Zinc Selenide
The laser scan lens consists of coated zinc selenide. This material is hazardous. Avoid contact and take care to
not damage the lens, which may release the zinc selenide as a particulate.
To prevent personal injury when handling these substances, always wear protective clothing and rubber gloves.
In case of contact with the laser scan lens with the skin, wash the respective part of skin with water and soap
immediately. Avoid any surface scratches of the laser scan lens. Do not inhale material particles. In the event
of a broken laser scan lens, carefully pack the fragments into a closed plastic bag and return to for disposal.
Lens Damage
As with all optical components the laser scan lens is an object of highest precision and sophisticated design!
Slightest damages of the surface might (in the long term) result in unusability of the component or reduction
of marking quality.
Avoid any surface scratches of the laser scan lens. Remove any contaminants using a lint- free optics cleaning
cloth and isopropyl alcohol or acetone.
Ensure that no contaminants enter the marking head while cleaning the laser scan lens!

Rescue Stick Procedure
When a laser with Bifrost board has power but will not boot, the remedy is to reload the CMark software. However, the only way
to load CMark is via the TCS+ interface which runs directly from the Bifrost board. This conundrum of needing to reload software
because laser will not boot, but not being able to reload software because the laser will not boot can be rectified by using the Res -
cue Stick procedure.
The Rescue Stick procedure is intended to remove potentially corrupt CMark files from the laser and to install a manufacturer
level software called PlexMark. Plexmark is only enough of the operating system to allow the user to connect and load CMark
through TCS+. Plexmark does not include CMark, therefore after using the Rescue Stick you must load CMark upon first suc -
cessful boot.
l The Rescue Stick is a user- supplied USB drive (minimum 4 GB) to which is written a bootable .ISO image file.
l The Rescue action removes from the Bifrost board all CMark files, including Jobs, Fonts, SARs, etc. This is why creating
backups periodically is an important preventative maintenance.
l The Rescue action installs Plexmark software (just enough software to boot), not CMark. You must load CMark to the sys -
tem after a successful boot.
l The Rescue action does not erase the System Database, which is saved on an EPPROM on the Bifrost board, though the
configuration of the laser system is not visible until CMark has been loaded.

Print Quality Issues
When determining the source of print quality defects, it is important to first investigate:
l Is the defect consistent? If not, then further investigate transport or inconsistent product movement. For example, an
incorrectly glued box flap causes a single bad code because it was out of focus for that product. Or, if every 12th product
has a code defect, this periodic inconsistent bad code quality could be caused by a defect in conveyor movement.
l Is the issue caused by incorrect speed or encoder setup? If an incorrect encoder setup is suspected, use the Compensation
Job template to verify.
l If a print quality issue is consistent and incorrect Mark Settings are suspected, then continue to Mark Settings
Troubleshooting.
Mark Settings Troubleshooting
Before adjusting any Mark Settings ensure:
l The lens is clean
l Working distance is correct
l The laser head is perpendicular to product
When adjusting Mark Settings only adjust one setting at a time.
