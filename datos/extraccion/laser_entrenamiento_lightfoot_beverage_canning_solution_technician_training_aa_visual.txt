Product Marking Techniques
Unlike an ink or pigment based coders that apply a new material onto a substrate, lasers mark on products by altering the sub -
strate itself.
Ablation
Ablation is the removal of a layer to reveal contrasting layer below. The topmost layer of film or paint on the substrate is vapor -
ized.

Thermal or Chemical Change
In a thermal/chemical change technique, light and heat from the beam interact with the substrate to alter it at the surface
without changing its texture or removing substrate. On metals this is occurs as a function of surface oxides forming on the sub -
strate surface at the point of beam contact. Similarly on plastics, the beam spurs molecules of the substrate to react and change
color. If the plastic does not react, dyes or additives may be added to the plastic medium before formation. The beam will react
with those additives during marking.

Physical Change - Surface Modification
Lasers may mark on substrates by burning away substrate to a target depth (in the tens of microns). A trench is left behind in the
shape of the marked characters or graphics. Substrates that are too thin for engraving (e.g. plastic bottles) may simply be mod -
ified at the surface causing the formation of bubbles that raise up rather than leave behind a trench. Glass does not burn and is
marked by crazing , or shattering, at the point of beam contact.
Marks in Glass Substrates Sharpen Over Time
Since marks on glass substrates rely on crazing and not vaporization, the removed substrate does not always
immediately fall away. This means that at first a mark on the glass substrate may appear faint but will
sharpen as the substrate jostles on a conveyor and the tiny shards fall away.
Engraving
When engraving, a superficial incision removes a few microns of material. This occurs via melting/vaporization (metal), partial
burning (thermoplastic), or crazing (glass).

Substrate Modification
When marking by substrate modification, the beam causes an additive effect on the substrate rather than a reductive one as
occurs in engraving. The surface is momentarily softened and then resolidifies. While softened, impurities such as oxides or
gasses are released that alter the color or texture of the substrate.

How Does a Laser Work?
Laser beams are the product of energy discharged from atoms as photons. This energy emits as electrons orbiting the nucleus of
the atoms decay to a lower energy level. The energy that formerly kept that electron moving at a higher orbit converts to a
photon.
Emissions may be spontaneous or stimulated . Spontaneous emissions occur naturally based on the interaction of excited atoms
in the electromagnetic field in their environment but are incoherent. Stimulated emissions occur when an incoming photon of a
specific frequency interacts with an excited atomic electron causing it to decay to a lower energy level. This interaction is called
absorption .
Ordinarily absorption produces no photons as the electrons simply raise to a higher energy level. Lasers work because the envir -
onment inside the beams source is such that instead of raising to a higher energy level, electrons instead decay and the dif -
ference in energy is released as a second photon of the same phase, frequency, polarization, and direction of travel.

Laser Wavelengths
Lasers beams, being light and therefore a form of electromagnetic radiation, can be produced at varying wavelengths on the
EM spectrum .
Wavelength is the distance between one peak or crest of a light wave and the next peak or crest. It is equal to
the speed of the wave divided by its frequency, and to the speed of a wave times its period.
Electromagnetic radiation has varying effects on people and things. The radio waves, light waves, and X Rays are all
EM radiation. Radio Waves are harmless to people, light is dangerous only at some exposures and wavelengths, and X Rays are
dangerous at more than minimal occasional exposure.
F IGURE 1- 10: Where laser radiation wavelengths fall on the electromagnetic spectrum.
The primary wavelengths of laser radiation for current commercial applications fall within the Ultraviolet (UV), visible, and
infrared ranges of the spectrum. UV laser radiation wavelengths range between 180 and 400 nm. Visible light wavelengths range
between 400 and 700 nm. The infrared range of the EM spectrum consists of radiation at wavelengths between 700 nm and 1
mm.

Radiation Exposure Hazard - Eye
F IGURE 1- 11: Illustrated visual impact of eye damage caused by laser radiation.
To understand the possible health effects, it is important to understand the functions of the major parts of the human eye.
The Human Eye
The cornea is the transparent layer of tissue covering the eye. Damage to the outer cornea may be uncomfortable (like a gritty
feeling) or painful, but will usually heal quickly. Damage to deeper layers of the cornea may cause permanent injury.
F IGURE 1- 12: Parts of the human eye.
18
Rev AA

The cornea is a clear, dome- shaped surface that covers the front of the eye. It is the first and most powerful lens in the eye's
optical system. To keep it transparent the cornea contains no blood vessels. Tears that flow over it and aqueous humor in the
chamber behind it keep it nourished. When you hear of eye transplants, it is the cornea that is being replaced. The cornea can be
damaged by accidents, infections, and genetic defects.
The pupil is the black circle in the center of the eye, and its primary function is to monitor the amount of light that comes into the
eye. When there is a lot of light, the pupil contracts to keep the light from overwhelming the eye. When there is very little light, the
pupil expands so it can soak up as much as possible.
The iris is the colored part of the eye. Although it might seem purely cosmetic, the iris actually functions to adjust the size of the
pupil. It has muscles that contract or expand depending on the amount of light the pupil needs to process images.
The lens exists behind the pupil and is responsible for allowing your eyes to focus on small details like words in a book. The lens is
in a constant state of adjustment as it becomes thinner or thicker to accommodate the detailed input it receives. With age, the
lens loses a lot of its elasticity which often results in cataracts and presbyopia, i.e. nearsightedness, because the lens cannot
adjust as well to its surroundings as it used to.
The vitreous humour is a gel- like substance that helps to keep the eyeball in its proper, circular shape. This is the area in the eye
where floaters develop as pieces of the vitreous humor clump together and cast shadows onto the retina. With age, the vitreous
humor begins to shrink and can cause problems like posterior retinal detachment or retinal tears.
The retina is the area at the back of the eye that receives the refined, visual message from the front of the eye, and it transmits
that visual message to the brain using electrical signals.
The sclera is the white part of the eye, and its main function is to provide strength, structure, and protection for the eye. The
sclera contains blood vessels that can tell an eye doctor a lot about the overall state of your health.

die. The resulting blind spot will destroy the central vision of this eye.

Radiation Exposure Hazard - Skin
Skin is the largest organ of the body and, as such, is at the greatest risk for coming in contact with the laser beam. The head,
hands, and arms are the most likely skin surfaces to be exposed.
22
Rev AA

l Stratum Corneum (Dead Layer)
The stratum corneum is the outermost layer. It consists of flattened, dead epidermal cells that protect the living tissue from
water loss, injury from physical objects, and radiant energy. The thickness is 8- 20 μm except on the soles of the feet and the
palms of the hands where it is 500- 600 μm.
l Epidermis
The epidermis is the outermost layer of living tissue, where the tanning process takes place. It has a relatively uniform thick -
ness of about 50- 150 μm.
l Dermis Corium
The dermis corium is made up largely of connective tissue which gives the skin its elasticity and supportive strength.
Included in this layer are nerve cells, blood vessels, and lymphatic glands. The thickness of this layer varies over the body
from 1- 4 mm.
l Subcutaneous Tissue
The subcutaneous tissue is made up mostly of fatty tissue serving as insulation and as a shock absorption medium. The
thickness of this layer varies according to the area of the body as well as from person to person.
Dermal Damage by Radiation Varies by Wavelength
Laser effects on tissue depend on the power density of the incident beam, absorption of tissues at the incident wavelength, time
beam is held on tissue, and the effects of blood circulation and heat conduction in the affected area.

Immediate Effects of Exposure
The immediate effect of exposure to laser light above the biological damage threshold is normally tissue burns. Injury to the skin
can be caused by either temperature elevation in skin tissues or from a photochemical effect (e.g., "sunburn") from excessive
levels of actinic ultraviolet radiation.

Delayed Effects of Exposure
The possibility of adverse effects from repeated or chronic laser irradiation to the skin has been suggested, although it is normally
discounted. Only optical radiation in the ultraviolet region of the spectrum has been shown to cause long- term, delayed effects
such as accelerated skin aging and skin cancer. At present, laser safety standards for exposure of the skin attempt to take these
adverse effects into account.
Burns, skin cancer and acceleration of skin aging are possible from exposure to laser radiation. Chronic exposure can cause
increased pigmentation.

Minimum temperature at which a liquid will burn. The Flashpoint for Methyl Ethyl Keytone (MEK) 73°F.

l Bonding
Connecting two objects or pieces of equipment with a conductive cable to maintain the same electrical potential between

Classes of Fire
Fires are classified as one of four types.
Class A - Ordinary Combustibles
Most common fire. A burning house is a class A fire.
Class B - Flammable Liquids
Liquid combustibles such as paint strippers, solvents, fuels, oil, or grease.
Class C - Electrical Equipment
Additional danger from electrical shock. Power equipment OFF immediately.
Class D - Combustible Metals
Combustible metals such as magnesium, aluminum, and metallic sodium. Metals burn easier in powder or chip form.
DO NOT apply water to a Class D fire as this can result in an explosion.

Fire Prevention & Safety
l Always store flammable liquids in approved cabinets.
l Never operate the laser system on unattended material.
l Always keep the area around the machine clean and free of clutter, combustible materials, explosives, or volatile solvents
such as acetone, alcohol, or gasoline.
l Always keep a properly maintained and inspected fire extinguisher on hand. A Halotron fire extinguisher or a multi- purpose
dry- chemical fire extinguisher. The Halotron extinguisher emits a clean, easily removable substance that is not harmful to
the mechanics or wiring of the laser system. Other dry chemical extinguisher can sometimes emit a sticky, corrosive powder
that is very difficult to clean up.
l Always use a fume extractor when vector printing.
l Many materials have the potential to burst suddenly into flames – even materials with which you might be very familiar.
Utilizing the Air Assist feature will reduce the instance of flare- ups when working with flammable materials such as acrylic
and wood.
l Keep your laser system clean - Build up of cutting and engraving residue and debris is dangerous and can create a fire haz -
ard in its own right. These small pieces present a very dangerous fire hazard, especially if they are allowed to collect over
time. Since most users cut wood and acrylic, these small pieces that fall into the conveyor, extractor nozzle or build up
around the lens act just like kindling and can ignite and start a fire.
l Lastly, make sure the exhaust blower you are using receives proper maintenance. Periodically clean the exhaust blower and
duct system to remove built- up debris. If you detect odor while engraving, or smoke in the cabinet is visible in the area of
the lens carriage, inspect the exhaust system for leaks and obstructions. Inspect and clean the exhaust ports in your
machine to ensure there are no obstructions within the machine itself. Use a wire brush to clean the plenum and exhaust
port of your machine.

Fume Exposure First Aid
Symptoms of exposure to this product are headaches, dizziness, nausea, difficult breathing, vomiting and diarrhea. In all cases
of overexposure get medical attention immediately. Take victim to a doctor or emergency treatment facility.
In Case of Inhalation
l Move person to fresh air.
l If person is not breathing, call 911 or an ambulance; give artificial respiration, preferably by
mouth- to- mouth, if possible.
l Keep warm and make sure person can breathe freely.
l Call a poison control center or doctor for further treatment advice.

Specular vs Diffuse Reflection
F IGURE 1- 27: The difference between light reflected on different surfaces.
Reflection off of smooth surfaces such as mirrors or a calm body of water leads to a type of reflection known as specular reflec -
tion . Reflection off of rough surfaces such as clothing, paper, and the asphalt roadway leads to a type of reflection known as dif -
fuse reflection . Whether the surface is microscopically rough or smooth has a tremendous impact upon the subsequent
reflection of a beam of light. The diagram below depicts two beams of light incident upon a rough and a smooth surface.
A light beam can be thought of as a bundle of individual light rays which are traveling parallel to each other. Each individual
light ray of the bundle follows the law of reflection. If the bundle of light rays is incident upon a smooth surface, then the light
rays reflect and remain concentrated in a bundle upon leaving the surface. On the other hand, if the surface is microscopically
rough, the light rays will reflect and diffuse in many different directions.
Specular reflections are mirror- like reflections and can reflect close to 100% of the incident light. Flat surfaces will not change a
fixed beam diameter only the direction. Convex surfaces will cause beam spreading, and concave surfaces will make the beam
converge.
Diffuse reflections result when surface irregularities scatter light in all directions. The specular nature of a surface is dependent
upon the wavelength of incident radiation. A specular surface is one that has a surface roughness less than the wavelength of
the incident light. A very rough surface is not specular to visible light but might be to IR radiation of 10.6 µm from a CO2 laser.
Why a rough Surface Diffuses a Light Beam
For each type of reflection, each individual ray follows the law of reflection. However, the roughness of the material means that
each individual ray meets a surface which has a different orientation. The normal line at the point of incidence is different for dif -
ferent rays. Subsequently, when the individual rays reflect off the rough surface according to the law of reflection, they scatter in
different directions. The result is that the rays of light are incident upon the surface in a concentrated bundle and are diffused
upon reflection.
Counter Measures
l Make sure that the working distance is set correctly before marking any reflecting material. Use a weakly reflecting material
for testing, e.g. laminated paper.
l When marking reflecting material try not to mark in the center of the marking field. Position the product so that the mark -
ing is in the edge of the marking field.
l When setting the laser power start with the maximum value and reduce the power until the marking result is satisfying. If
no marking is visible at maximum power the material cannot be marked using this laser.

Nominal Hazard Zone
Nominal Hazard Zone (NHZ) is a distance within which the irradiance of a beam is greater than the MPE. Besides being specific
to a given wavelength and time of exposure, a different NHZ can be defined for the beam’s path to your eye ( direct viewing),
specular reflectance, and diffuse reflectance.
The NHZ is a practical definition; it has a specific shape around your particular laboratory apparatus. For instance, assuming
your lab has no windows and a solid door, the NHZ will in the worst case scenario be the area (e.g. square footage) of the lab
itself. In other words, the NHZ for you will be derived at the end of your safety calculations, and thereafter will be most useful to
you for planning control measures in you laboratory.

Installation
Overview - Shutterlock vs Interlock
The AL- 81833 Safety Lock Kit contains three switches and Y- connectors to make installation of the daisy chain of switches
simple. The only place where considerations are required is at the termination at the laser system.
Wiring as a Door Circuit (Shutterlock) when a FASS is installed will meet Performance Level D standards and have the fol -
lowing behavior:
l When any connected access door of the 7020 or 7030 is opened, the laser system will close the shutter but the laser
source will still be energized.
l The customer will benefit from quick recovery after access door is closed.
l The customer still has to Start marking again, but there is no delay.
Wiring as an Emergency Circuit (Interlock) when a FASS is installed will meet Performance Level E standards and have the fol -
lowing behavior:
l When any connected access door of the 7020 or 7030 is opened, the laser system will close the shutter and the beam
source is de- energized.
l The customer must press the manual reset button after the access door is closed.
l The Interlock is closed again on the trailing edge of the reset signal.
l There will be a 5- 10 second delay as the beam source is re- engergized.
l The customer must Start marking again.

Laser Configuration, the Leader laser will send vector information for the characters that should be printed by the Follower. The
Leader (Laser 1) and Follower (Laser 2) print alternating characters in the message to create one complete message as shown in
Figure 4- 1 below .

Focus Finder Activation
The 7340/7440 laser system is equipped with a pilot laser built into the laser source. This focus finder can help you to find the cor -
rect focal distance for marking by rapidly and alternately projecting two images, a circle and a dot, which appear to the human
eye simultaneously. As the marking head approaches the correct focal distance, the dot moves closer to the circle. At the correct
distance, the dot should appear centered inside the circle.
The Focus Finder feature can only be activated through TCS+ when you are logged in at a level that has access to edit the Line
Setup. Not all users are allowed to access this option by default.
Wear appropriate eye protection
Use of the Focus Finder feature requires that the laser source is armed. Though the pilot laser used in the Focus
Finder is Class II, when the laser source is armed it cannot be guaranteed that there is no Class IV beam of
wavelength 1,055- 1,075 escaping. Therefore eye protection for the 1,055- 1,075 wavelength should be worn or
the marking head should be in approved enclosure whenever the Focus Finder or pilot laser are in use.

Adjust Pilot Laser Modes
Only available in CMark 4.9 and up
This functionality is only available on lasers with CMark operating system version 4.9 or newer. Older versions
of CMark do not include this functionality.
On laser models equipped with a pilot laser, different patterns can be drawn with the pilot laser to demonstrate the currently
defined marking region. Not all Videojet lasers have a pilot laser installed. A pilot laser is an optional accessory that is either part
of the beam source itself or a separate piece that is installed into the head. The pilot laser emits a visible red beam that can dis -
play either the message to be marked or a number of different patterns but does not actually mark the product. The pilot laser is
useful for setup in static applications as the image displayed by the red pilot laser strobes while on, but does not wait for encoder
pulses or triggers. Because the pilot laser is not based on encoder pulses or trigger signals, it is not often used in mark on the fly
applications.
7340/7440 Pilot Laser Precautions
The 7340 and 7440 lasers use a pilot laser beam of 600- 700 nm that is generated by the beam source. There is
no way to completely guarantee that only the pilot laser beam and not the real laser beam is emitted since
they are generated by the same component. When using the pilot laser on 7340/7440, use the same care as
would be used with the regular 1064 nm beam including beam shielding or appropriate safety glasses and
treat it as a Class 4 laser.
Pilot Laser Care
In all Videojet lasers other than the 7340/7440, the pilot laser beam is a 600- 700 nm and is considered Class 2
laser radiation. This laser radiation may cause damage to the eye when staring into the beam.
It is safe for the eye if natural defense mechanisms (blink reflex, turning the head) are not suppressed when
unintentionally looking into the beam.
Use appropriately rated laser safety glasses or beam shield enclosure to prevent eye damage.

Temporarily Use a Single Laser
It may occur in a Multi Laser Configuration that one laser fails and the expected fix time could be too long for the customer to
be without the laser. In such a case as this, it is possible to use only one of the lasers in the dual laser setup. If it is the Follower
laser that fails, then the changeover to using only the Leader will merely be a few steps. If the Leader laser has failed and use of
only Follower is desired, then the changeover will be much more involved and time consuming.
Speed will be reduced by half!
Dropping down to a single laser will reduce the speed with which the system is able to mark by half. The cus -
tomer’s line must be slowed to accommodate this, but doing so would allow for some continued production
while spare parts are sent or the issue with the affected laser is diagnosed and repaired.
Follower Laser Failure
If Follower laser fails and cannot be recovered in a timely manner, then it is only necessary to disable the Multi- Laser Con -
figuration on the Leader Laser to use the Leader on its own.

Laser Unit Settings.
2. Press Main Menu .

4. Select the Multi Laser Configuration tab.
5. Toggle OFF Multi Laser Enable .

Troubleshooting
Leader Laser Failure
The following steps are not exhaustive and these steps will vary based on the complexity of the application. Use the following as
a boilerplate checklist of what might need to be moved, adjusted, or installed to temporarily re- purpose the Follower laser as a
stand alone marking unit.
To use only Follower in multi laser installation:
1. Copy Jobs, Mark Settings, and Line Setup from the Leader laser to USB.
2. Disable Multi Laser Configuration in Laser Unit Settings |  Laser Configuration .
- OR-
If MIC board present, then disable through Laser Unit Settings | External Interface .
3. Shut down both lasers and disconnect power to both units.
4. If the Follower laser does not already have a MIC board, remove the Leader MIC board and install it into the Follower.
5. Move the TU430, if present, from the Leader to the Follower.
6. If the application uses only an encoder and photo eye for IO devices, move the wires for these items from the Leader to
the Follower.
- OR-
In cases where several additional IOs are also wired into Leader’s customer interface, the following actions are sug -
gested:
6.1. Remove the cable gland frames holding cables coming into Leader laser Customer Interface (one on rear of drawer
and one on rear of laser).
6.2. Remove the top of the cable gland frame of on the supply unit (leaving the cable gland plate from the rear of the
drawer assembled).
6.3. Remove any connectors on the Customer Interface that have wiring connected from outside the laser (Photo eye,
encoder, ready to mark, etc.). Leave wires attached to the devices. Just unlock connectors and remove from board.
6.4. Slide the blocks with cables through them out of frame.
